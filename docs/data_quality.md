In my data first-generation students seem to have equality and be fairly distributed on the surface. It breaks down so they get a mentor at the same rate as everyone else: roughly 67% versus 66%. But if we dig into the data and look at another level deeper, then we see there are some important findings.

For first-generation students, with an N of 231, 67% have a mentor but only 5.8% have a mentor in their own department. If we look at non-first-generation students, with an N of 269, we see that 66% have a mentor but almost 13% have a mentor that's in the student's own department. First-gen students get a mentor from their own department: less than half of what non-first-gen students get. The assumption should be that first-gen students would get mentors in their own department more often because first-generation students would need more help individually. That's the Amsterdam pattern in my data. From a brief perusal of the data, it looks to be fair but when you try to dig down and understand what's happening, you can see that there's bias inside the data.
